Part One

What Matobev is

Matobev is a talent-discovery platform for African footballers. A player uploads a highlight video from their phone; software analyses how they move and produces a rating; scouts search, watch, shortlist and make contact.

The problem it addresses

Talent in African football is abundant and badly distributed. A gifted seventeen-year-old in Kisumu is not short of ability — they are short of anyone credible seeing them play. Discovery runs on physical presence and personal networks: being at the right academy, knowing someone, being able to travel to a trial. Each of those is a filter on circumstance rather than on ability.

That vacuum is filled by exploitation. Fake agents charge families for trials that do not exist, for "registration fees", for travel that never happens. It is one of the most reliable ways to take money from a family that has very little, because the thing being sold — hope — is the one thing they will pay for.

The product's core bet

If a player can be seen and assessed from a phone video, then discovery stops depending on geography and connections. The rating is not the product. Being visible to someone real is the product. The rating is the mechanism that makes a stranger worth a scout's attention.

What makes it different

Not the analysis — the honesty about it. The engine currently measures a handful of attributes and is frequently unsure. Rather than hiding that behind a confident number, the app states it: every score carries a confidence, low-confidence values are visibly marked, and the overall rating is weighted so an uncertain measurement counts for less.

Why this is the whole strategy

Ratings drive real decisions about young people who have very little. A confident number that is wrong sends a scout on a wasted journey and tells a teenager something false about themselves. On a scale users read fluently from football games — where 99 is world class — an unqualified 16 is not a statistic, it is a verdict. The platform's long-term credibility with scouts rests entirely on never having overstated what it knows.

How a player moves through it

  1. Arrive — browse real players before signing up; no wall in front of the value.
  2. Register — four required fields only: name, date of birth, nationality, position.
  3. Upload — a highlight from the camera roll, tagging themselves in one frame.
  4. Get rated — analysis runs; the result arrives with its confidence attached.
  5. Be seen — appear in scout search and on the improvement leaderboard.
  6. Return — because a scout viewed the profile, the rating moved, or the week had something in it.

How a scout moves through it

  1. Verify — submit credentials; unverified accounts see less.
  2. Set preferences — positions, countries, age range, minimum rating.
  3. Discover — recommendations built from those preferences and real activity, never a fabricated reason.
  4. Assess — open a profile, watch the footage, read the attribute breakdown and its confidence.
  5. Shortlist — save to folders, add private notes, compare side by side.
  6. Act — message the player, or post a trial and receive applications.

Part Three

How I would design it

The architecture is strong. The visual identity is not yet an identity — it is a set of reasonable defaults that do not agree with each other.

The central problem: the logo and the app are different brands

The logo is a teal-to-navy mark. The app is built on electric blue with a gold accent. Measured from the logo's own pixels against the app's tokens:

ElementColourHueWhere it lives
Logo — dominant#00A0B0185°The mark, everywhere it appears
Logo — ground#002040210°The mark's dark figures
App primary#1A6DFF218°Every button, link and active state
App accent#FFD54F46°Ratings, highlights
Why 33 degrees is the worst possible distance

Teal at 185° and blue at 218° are close enough to read as a mistake and far enough apart to clash. If they were identical they would be coherent; if they were opposite they would be a deliberate pairing. Sitting a third of the way between reads as two people who never spoke. Every screen in the app currently argues with its own logo.

The direction: build outward from the mark

The logo is the one asset that will not change, so it should decide the palette rather than be decorated around. That gives three moves:

  • Teal becomes primary. Buttons, links, active states, focus rings — everything currently electric blue.
  • Navy becomes the dark ground and the ink. It already is in the logo; it should be the app's darkest surface and its heading colour.
  • Gold stays, and gets a job. Teal at 186° and gold at 46° are near-complementary — they reinforce rather than compete. Gold should mean exactly one thing: achievement. Ratings, leaderboard positions, milestones. Nothing else.

What each colour is allowed to mean

The current palette's real weakness is not the hues, it is that blue means six different things — primary action, link, active tab, information, selection, and brand. When one colour means everything it means nothing, and users stop reading it as a signal.

ColourMeans exactlyNever used for
TealSomething you can do, or where you areStatus, achievement, decoration
GoldSomething earned — a rating, a rank, a milestoneButtons, warnings, links
NavyStructure — headings, dark surfaces, the card groundInteractive elements

Four principles I would hold the design to

1. Depth must survive dark mode

Shadows are invisible on a dark ground, so hierarchy there has to come from lighter surfaces. The rule is simple and must be verified numerically rather than by eye: every level of the stack must be measurably lighter than the one beneath it. Hex codes do not sort by perceived lightness, and this exact ordering has been silently inverted twice in this codebase already.

2. Uncertainty is a visual property, not a footnote

A low-confidence rating should look different from a confident one at a glance — muted, marked, and never eligible to win a comparison. This is the product's differentiator and it belongs in the type and colour system, not in help text nobody opens.

3. One celebratory moment, and only one

The arrival of a rating is the emotional peak. That is where motion, haptics and gold belong. Everywhere else should be quiet, because an app that celebrates constantly celebrates nothing — and because these users are on budget Android hardware where animation costs frames.

4. Designed for the actual device and hand

Mid-range Android, outdoors, in sunlight, often one-handed, frequently on metered data. That argues for higher contrast than a design system default, a 17px body minimum, primary actions in the lower half of the screen, and never spending a megabyte before telling the user what it will cost.

The trap worth naming

Contrast is symmetric — white-on-teal and teal-on-white are necessarily the same ratio. The distinction that actually matters is which surface, not which is foreground: a colour that works on white will usually fail on navy, and the reverse. Gold is the clearest case in this palette — 11.62 : 1 on navy and 1.23 : 1 on white, the same colour, unusable in one of them.

The honest assessment

Matobev looks like a real app that has not been used yet. The engineering is genuinely strong — access control enforced in the database, no fabricated data anywhere, confidence surfaced rather than hidden, real child-safety controls. But until this week a scout could not play a player's video, the back button vanished on a light photo, and the leaderboard offered scouts a filter on a position they do not have. None of those are subtle; all three survived because the app was verified by typechecks and bundles rather than by someone using it as a scout. The gap is not polish or architecture. It is that nobody has walked each role end to end.
